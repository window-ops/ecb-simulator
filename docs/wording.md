# Wording: the Democracy 4 standard

[Back to Developing](developing.md)

The game's text follows the way political strategy games such as Democracy 4 (Positech Games) word their interface. This page calls that the Democracy 4 standard. It applies to every card, option, button, notice and help page. A text that does not follow it is a bug.

## What the standard asks

- **Options are commands**: an option or a button says what the player does, starting with a verb: "Raise to 4.25%", "Adopt", "Pressure governments", "Support targeted help for households".
- **The words of the political game**: the player pressures governments, adopts or rejects policies, and supports or opposes proposals. Groups give or withdraw support; voters approve or disapprove. The same word always means the same thing, and the glossary defines it once.
- **Plain cause and effect**: an option's text says what happens, in the order it happens: "Borrowing costs more: inflation falls, and so do output and jobs." There are no idioms or metaphors.
- **Figures with their units**: percentages, points, years in full and amounts in euro, as in "a spread of 4.2 points" or "1.3 billion euro".
- **Short**: a card title has two to six words. An option label has at most about eight; the option's own text carries the detail.
- **A neutral voice**: cards say what happened and who asks for what. The game does not praise or blame the player's choices; the figures and the people in the game react.
- **The bank in the third person, the player addressed directly**: cards speak of "the bank" and "the president". Buttons, notices and help speak to the player ("You may replace one advisor a year").

## How it differs from everyday language

Everyday and journalistic English leans on idioms, figures of speech and words with several meanings. The standard keeps to one plain word for each act, so a player can compare options at a glance and a translation stays exact.

| Everyday wording | The standard | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Press governments | Pressure governments | "Press" also means the newspapers |
| Back a guarantee of work | Support a guarantee of work | "Back" is an idiom; "support" is the game's word |
| Look through the price rise | Treat the price rise as temporary | A figure of speech, said plainly |
| Signal early rate rises | Announce rate rises soon | "Signal" hides what the bank does |
| Take a slow approach | Delay the digital euro | Says what is delayed |
| Let the words stand | Leave the remark uncorrected | An idiom, said plainly |
| Tighten the rules | Make the rules stricter | A metaphor, said plainly |
| Each euro cut then costs more than a euro of output | Each euro of spending cut lowers output by more than a euro | The cause and its effect, in order |
| Hold at 4.00% | Keep at 4.00% | "Hold" is market jargon; "keep" is the plain word |
| Give guidance | Announce low rates for a long time | Says what the bank announces |
| Wait | Make no change for now | Says what the bank does |
| Fix it quietly | Repair it without a full report | An idiom, said plainly |
| Wait for prices to settle | Wait for prices to fall | "Settle" hides the direction |
| Stay silent | Make no statement | An act of the bank, said as one |
| Put employment first | Prioritize employment | One verb for setting a priority, the same in every text |

## Checking a text

1. Does each option start with what the player does, in a precise verb (keep, raise, cut, announce, publish, ask, support, oppose)?
2. Is every political act one of the game's words (pressure, adopt, support, oppose, approve)?
3. Would the sentence read the same to someone who has never heard the idiom?
4. Does every figure have its unit?
5. Is the card title six words or fewer, and each label about eight or fewer?
